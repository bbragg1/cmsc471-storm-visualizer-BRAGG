2017 Storm and Precipitation Tracker - Brady Bragg

In 2017, a string of big storms hit the United States. Harvey dropped almost four feet of rain on parts of Texas. Irma swept across Florida. Maria hit Puerto Rico just as this data ends. This map shows daily rain and snow at about 1,500 weather stations from January 1 to September 21, 2017, so you can see where each storm hit hardest.

Try this: pick Hurricane Harvey from the Storm menu, switch to Running total, and drag the slider. Hover over any station for its details.

Map: each dot is a weather station, colored by the amount for the selected day (or the running total). A filled gray dot means a reading of zero. A hollow ring means the station sent no reading that day. Scroll to zoom, drag to pan, and hover a station for details.

Bar chart: each bar is one day of 2017. Its height and color show the most rain (or snow) any single station got that day, so big storms stand out as tall spikes. The white line is the selected day. Drag across the bars to pick a date range, or click a bar to jump to that day.

Write-up

The data

I used the Daily Weather in the U.S., 2017 dataset from the course, which comes from NOAA's GHCN-Daily records. Each row is one weather station on one day, from January 1 to September 21, 2017. I focused on precipitation since that's where the big storms show up. The main columns are PRCP (daily rain in inches) and SNOW (daily snowfall in inches). The tooltip also shows SNWD (snow on the ground) and WSF5 (fastest wind gust in mph).

The raw file is 37 MB, which is too slow for a web page, so a short Python script (prep_data.py) cuts it down to about 9 MB. It keeps only the 50 states, Puerto Rico and the Virgin Islands, removes impossible values, and splits the data into a station list and a readings list. One bad value it removes is Teterboro, NJ reporting 68 inches of snow on March 13, when stations nearby got about 7.

Two gaps in the data matter. First, a blank means the station didn't report, which isn't the same as zero, so the map shows those stations as hollow rings. Second, after September 17 only about 650 of the usual 1,550 stations report. So when the Puerto Rico and Virgin Islands stations go silent on September 19, the day Maria hit, I can't say for sure it was the storm. Irma is clearer, since the St. Thomas station went quiet from September 6 to 10 while everything else kept working.

Design decisions

I drew each station as its own dot instead of shading whole states. Storm rain is very local. Harvey dropped 47 inches on Port Arthur while El Paso got none, and a state average would hide that. I thought about a hexagon grid too, but dots let you hover over a real place.

Rain uses a threshold color scale that goes from green for drizzle, through blue and purple, to red for downpours. The breaks are close together at the low end and spread out at the top (0.01 up to 60 inches), since about half of rainy days get 0.2 inches or less but the wettest day got 26. A linear scale would have made almost every dot look the same. The same breaks work for daily amounts and running totals, so a color always means the same amount. Snow gets its own scale from steel blue to white, and anything 18 inches or more gets a soft glow.

I went with a dark theme because the bright colors stand out more on it. Zero rain is a dim gray that fades into the map, and the darkest reds get a thin light ring so they don't disappear.

For the bar chart I first tried the daily average across all stations, but the storms barely showed up. On Harvey's worst day the average was only 0.15 inches. The daily maximum shows each storm as a clear spike, even though one station can make a whole day look stormy.

Puerto Rico and the Virgin Islands aren't part of the standard US map projection, so I added a small inset box with its own projection. Irma and Maria both hit there, so leaving them out wasn't an option. I also considered giving each storm its own small map, but one map with a storm menu kept things readable and still lets you explore the rest of the year.

Interactions

Filtering and linking (Category I):

  • Date slider: picks the day shown on the map.
  • Storm menu: sets the slider to the storm's dates plus 3 days on each side, switches to rain or snow, zooms the map, and shows a card with details.
  • Rain / Snow and Daily / Running total toggles: change what the dots show.
  • Brushing and linking: dragging across the bar chart sets the slider range, and the map updates with it. Clicking a bar jumps to that day.

Exploration and elaboration (Category II):

  • Tooltips: hovering a station shows its name, elevation, that day's amount, running total, and wind gust.
  • Zoom and pan: scroll and drag on the map, or pick a storm to zoom to it. A Reset zoom button goes back to the full view.
  • Highlighting: the hovered station gets a white outline, days outside the range fade on the bar chart, and the inset turns yellow for Irma and Maria.
  • Storm cards: each storm shows its dates, where it hit, and its peak station.

Data sources and references

  • Weather data: Daily Weather in the U.S., 2017, provided by CMSC471. Original source is NOAA's GHCN-Daily.
  • State shapes: us-atlas (states-10m.json, v3.0.1) by Mike Bostock.

Development process

I did this project on my own, so I handled everything from the data cleaning to the write-up. I wasn't really keeping track of time, but it was probably around 10 to 12 hours. I started when fall break began on October 10 and worked a couple hours a day, with about 5 hours on the last day.

Cleaning the data took the most time. The file looked tidy at first, but it had stations from Canada and Guam mixed in, values that couldn't be real, and two different stations both named JACKSON. I found the bad values by sorting each column and comparing the top ones to nearby stations. I also checked each storm's dates against the rain peaks in the data. They all matched, though Emily was hard to confirm since regular Florida summer storms blur it. While checking Maria, I found the drop in reports after September 17, which changed how I describe that storm.

On the visualization side, keeping the storm menu, slider, and brush in sync was the trickiest part, since moving the brush from code fires the same event as a real drag. Zooming also took work, as the dots grew when zoomed in and the map projection can't place points out in the ocean. Getting the colors readable on a dark background took a few tries too.

Overall, the biggest lesson was to check the data before trusting the story. I expected Maria to be the big finish, but the data runs out right as it arrives. My favorite part is turning on the running total for Harvey and dragging the slider to watch Houston turn red.
